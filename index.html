01
THE PRACTICE

The useful pause.

Most regrettable replies are written in the first thirty seconds.

There is a small distance between what happens and what we do next. On a good day, we notice it. On a difficult day, we fill it immediately—with a reply, a judgement, a defence, a decision.

The pause is not passivity. It is the moment where impulse becomes choice. Even ninety seconds can change the quality of a conversation: read the message once, stand up, breathe, ask what outcome you actually want, then return.

A useful rule: when the emotion is loud, make the next move small. Draft the reply. Do not send it. Ask one clarifying question. Sleep on the decision that is trying to become permanent too quickly.

The best response is rarely the fastest one. It is the one you can still stand behind tomorrow.

THINKING WITH TOOLS

Questions AI cannot answer.

A tool can generate options. It cannot decide what should matter to you.

AI is remarkably good at making the blank page disappear. It can summarise, compare, draft and organise. But the hardest part of many decisions is not producing an answer. It is deciding which question deserves to be asked.

What trade-off are we willing to accept? Who carries the downside? What will still matter when the excitement wears off? Which choice fits the person—or organisation—we are trying to become?

Those are not information problems. They are judgement problems. A system can help us see more angles, but it cannot inherit responsibility for the direction we choose.

Use AI to widen the map. Keep the compass.

03
WORKING RHYTHM

Design the week before it designs you.

A calendar is not just a record of commitments. It is a statement of priorities.

Most weeks do not collapse because we lacked effort. They collapse because every open space quietly became available to someone else.

Try a fifteen-minute weekly reset. Before Monday begins, decide where the week needs depth, where it needs movement, and where it needs recovery. Then protect those choices before the requests arrive.

The goal is not to control every hour. It is to make sure your most important work does not have to survive on whatever energy remains.

A CRITICAL-THINKING ESCAPE ROOM

The editorial room.

Ajith left today’s final page locked inside a drawer. The obvious clues are mostly distractions.

You arrive before the editorial meeting. On the desk is a locked drawer. Inside it is the line that completes this edition.

The room contains a stopped clock, an upside-down book, a framed reminder, a plant, a lamp, a door—and one object almost everyone treats as background.

Inspect the room. Keep useful evidence. Question what appears fixed. The answer is not where everyone looks.

This is not a speed test. It is a test of assumptions. Everything happens inside the article; no new page opens.

A THOUGHT TO KEEP

The strength to revise.

Changing your mind is not proof that your old thinking was worthless.

We often defend a position long after the evidence has moved—not because the position is still useful, but because it has become attached to our identity.

A better question than “Was I wrong?” is “What can I see now that I could not see then?” That turns revision from an admission of failure into evidence of learning.

Strong convictions are useful. Stronger still is the ability to update them without treating every change as a loss of face.

You do not need to become less decisive. You need to become less trapped by yesterday’s decision.
